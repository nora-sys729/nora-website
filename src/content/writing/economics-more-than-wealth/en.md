When societies are organized for long periods around property, inheritance, bloodline, control, ownership, and competition, do these institutions gradually shape a way of seeing the world as something that can be owned?

One of the most common starting points in modern economics is that human wants are unlimited while resources are scarce. From this comes a familiar sequence: scarcity → choice → allocation → competition. It can easily produce a particular worldview: the world is a collection of scarce resources, while people are competitors, producers, consumers, and owners of those resources. But this economic definition hides a major assumption. It places “time” and “nature” outside the economic system. Things tend to acquire “economic value” only after they enter systems of human production and exchange. Measurement itself is one of civilization’s great capabilities and sources of potential. Yet if a society begins to value only what can be measured, that in itself may be a dangerous signal.

So I want to temporarily put aside the definition found in economics textbooks and look at something more fundamental. A tree draws what it needs from soil, water, and sunlight; it grows, reproduces, dies, and becomes part of other forms of life. Animals obtain food, reproduce, care for their young, and die. Humans do the same. At the most fundamental level, then, economics is not simply a market activity invented by humans. It is the process through which life sustains and reproduces itself within finite environments, exchanges energy and matter, creates relationships, and continues across time.

And this brings us to two dimensions that economics has historically underestimated: time and space.

Economic actors often have strong rights in the present without corresponding responsibilities to the future. A company may exist for only a few decades. A government may have only a few years in office. An investor may think only in terms of a few years. A person may live for several decades. But forests take centuries to grow. Soil can take thousands of years to form. Species evolve over hundreds of thousands or millions of years. Civilizations themselves may take thousands of years to develop.

Even from a human-centered perspective, the fact that future people have no vote is an important problem of time. Someone born one hundred years from now cannot vote today. They cannot sue the people living today. They cannot participate in today’s policy decisions. And yet we can decide what kind of world they will inherit. This means that people alive today possess a form of power that future people have no way to counterbalance.

If time asks us to respect the future, then space asks us to rethink our relationship with nature. I think we need to go further than the idea of “environmental protection.” The phrase itself can still imply that humans are the subjects and nature is an object that needs to be protected. We could understand it differently: humans are one form of life within an ecosystem. Nature is therefore not simply an “external environment.” It is a precondition for the existence of the economy itself.

We depend on soil for food, on the water cycle for life, and on forests, oceans, and countless ecosystems for conditions that we cannot create independently. We are not standing outside nature and using it. We are part of nature ourselves. Nature, therefore, should not be treated merely as an “externality” to be calculated after economic activity has taken place. It is one of the conditions that makes economic activity possible in the first place.

From “growth” to “continuity”: if we accept this, then we also need to rethink what economic growth means. A forest can be cut down, a mine can be exploited, and a polluted river can later be cleaned up — all of these activities can contribute to GDP. They may increase the numbers recorded in economic statistics, but that does not necessarily mean that we have created an equivalent amount of wealth.

Sometimes, we are simply converting natural wealth into monetary wealth. Sometimes, we are converting future wealth into wealth today. The question worth asking is: are we creating wealth, or are we consuming wealth?

Perhaps what we ultimately need to redefine is not only economic growth, but what we mean by genuine social progress. If a society possesses more wealth today while consuming its forests, soil, water, biodiversity, and the choices available to future generations, can we really say that it has become richer?

If a person accumulates enormous wealth during a lifetime but leaves no greater possibilities for those who come after them, can we really say that they have succeeded?

Perhaps we could try another definition. Success is not only about how much a person possesses when they leave this world. It is also about whether, when they leave, they have left the world with more possibilities for life than it had when they arrived.

Ultimately, we are only temporary inhabitants here. We inherit the world from the past, and we pass it on to the future.

Time reminds us that we are not the endpoint of this world.  
Space reminds us that we are not its owners either.  
We are simply one generation within it.
